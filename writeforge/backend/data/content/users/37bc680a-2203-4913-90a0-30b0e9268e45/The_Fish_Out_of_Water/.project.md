Tout commence at CERN. Ten honours students — volunteers, test subjects, people who said oui to something they only partially understood — were laid out in hospital beds inside a modified section of the collider, surrounded by machinery of considerable theoretical ambition. Whatever the experiment was intended to accomplish, it did not accomplish that. The machines discharged. The subjects were scattered across time — four centuries of it — though nobody knew this at the time, which is arguably the kindest aspect of the entire business.
The player characters found themselves on an uninhabited island in the Caribbean, in a century that predates their birth by roughly three hundred years. They had each other, whatever was in their pockets, and the dawning realisation that they possessed no skills applicable to survival in any historical period whatsoever. After twelve days — during which Thomas built a structurally sound shelter and Eleanor identified which of the local plants were actively attempting to kill them — the French privateer vessel Fish Out of Water found them and took them aboard with the particular hospitality that pirates extend to strangers washed up on beaches: provisional survival, contingent on usefulness.
This is where the story begins.
The premise is what it is: honours students with deep specialist knowledge placed in a world that predates their expertise by three centuries and has no particular interest in catching up. The tension between what they know and what they can do — and, more pressingly, what they manifestly cannot do — is the engine of the narrative. Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles, as a certain French satirist once wrote, with what we may now recognise as magnificent insincerity. Our scholars will discover the truth of that sentiment at regular intervals throughout the campaign.
