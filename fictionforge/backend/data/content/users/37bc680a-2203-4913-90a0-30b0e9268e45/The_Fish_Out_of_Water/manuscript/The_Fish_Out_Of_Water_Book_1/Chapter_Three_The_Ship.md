# Chapter Three: The Ship

The twelfth notch on the palm trunk was crooked, carved by a thumbtack from a biology textbook that had dissolved in the rain two days prior. Flies clustered at the corners of eyes, drawn to the salt crusted on skin, the acrid tang of empty stomachs that had not seen tinned fruit since the ninth day. The sea, a flat expanse of bleached blue, offered no mercy, only the occasional slap of a wave against the reef that had torn the hull of their charter catamaran twelve sunsets ago.

Liam, who had not shaved since the crash and looked more like a castaway from a 19th-century broadsheet than a third-year business major, shaded his eyes with a hand that trembled from hunger. “Ship.”

The word travelled fast, faster than the spark from the Bic lighter they had hoarded for the signal fire. Chloe, who had minored in maritime history and knew the difference between a brig and a sloop but chose to believe in tall ship festivals and historical reenactments, squinted at the horizon. “It’s a French privateer. Look at the three masts, the square rig. That’s a frigate, late 18th century. Replica, probably. Part of the Bastille Day celebrations in Nouméa?”

The narrator, who knew the precise cost of gunpowder in Marseille in 1724 and the name of the privateer’s captain—Jean-Luc Bisset, a man who had never seen a steam engine, let alone a charter catamaran—watched the ship anchor. The chain rattled, a thin, metallic scream that carried over the water, splashing into a sea that had not yet been charted by the British Admiralty. The Bourbon flag, white silk with gold fleurs-de-lis, fluttered at the stern, unbothered by the fact that it would be obsolete within seventy years.

“Light the fire,” Chloe said, already gathering the dry mangrove wood they had stacked behind the palm. “Before they sail past.”

Liam fumbled for the lighter. It's plastic gleamed, its harsh spark contrasting the ancient sea as smoke carried the acrid scent of modern desperation. His thumb slipped twice, the flint sparked, caught the dry palm fronds. Smoke billowed, grey and thick, smelling of burning sap and the plastic casing of the Bic, which had been manufactured in Lyon in 2023, three centuries after the ship’s crew had last set foot on dry land. The irony was not lost on the narrator, who had seen enough of human folly to recognise a closed loop when it presented itself: French fire, French ship, French folly, all circling a palm tree on an island that did not exist on any map printed after 1800.

The ship responded.

The longboat sliced through the turquoise shallows with a wet crunch of wood meeting water, each stroke sending up plumes of white foam that caught the afternoon light like scattered salt. Six oars dug deep, their blades churning the sea into a frothy lace of bubbles that hissed and popped around the hull, while the worn wooden thwarts creaked under the weight of men whose boots thudded rhythmically against the boat's bottom. The oarsmen's breathing came in measured grunts, their bodies swaying in practised unison like pendulums keeping time to an ancient maritime heartbeat.

Tricorn hats sat at jaunty angles atop heads wrapped in faded bandanas, the once-vibrant blue wool of their coats softened by salt and sun into the gray-blue of storm clouds, each garment patched and stained with the accumulated grime of months at sea. The fabric whispered against itself with every movement, scratchy against weathered skin, while leather belts creaked and metal buttons caught the light like scattered coins. These men smelled of tar and unwashed wool, of hardtack and the metallic tang of blood from knuckles split on rigging, their very presence carrying the musty perfume of the age of sail.

Muskets gleamed dully in the sun, their dark steel barrels reflecting light like wounded serpents, the polished walnut stocks worn smooth by countless hands and resting now on bony knees that bounced with each oar stroke. The flintlocks caught the rays, tiny mirrors flashing warnings, while powder horns dangled from shoulders with soft leather thuds, redolent of sulfur and the sharp bite of black powder that had settled into every pore of their clothing. Each weapon hummed with deadly potential, cold metal waiting to transform into thunder and flame.

The coxswain's voice cut through the rhythmic splashing, sharp as a cutlass, his commands echoing across the water as the longboat bore down upon the shore like a predatory bird, oars churning the sea into submission, the whole vessel alive with the promise of violence and the weight of centuries-old naval tradition pressing down upon both hunter and hunted.

Liam whooped, waving his cracked iPhone, the screen dead, the time frozen at 14:37, the moment the catamaran had struck the reef. “Over here! We’re American! Students!”

“Americains?” The coxswain, a man with a scar across his left cheek and a beard that housed more lice than a stable, shouted back in a dialect that would be unintelligible to a modern Parisian. “There is no such country. They are British spies, dressed in rags to trick us. Load the pans.”

Chloe stepped forward, her AP French 2 certificate clutched in her hand like a talisman. “Nous sommes des étudiants! Nous avons besoin d’aide! S’il vous plaît!”

The coxswain frowned, turned to the man next to him, who was checking the flint of his musket. “She speaks gibberish. A spy’s trick. Keep the hammers cocked.”

The students did not hear. They were wading into the surf, knee-deep, laughing, splashing, Liam hauling his bag—a spare t-shirt, a bottle of warm water, a half-eaten granola bar—above his head. Chloe adjusted her glasses, which had been repaired with tape, and smiled. “We’re going home. Pizza. Cold IPA. My bed. I’m never leaving the city again.”

The narrator knew better. Home, for them, was a continent that had not yet been named, a future that was three hundred years away. The privateer’s captain, Bisset, had a letter of marque from the King of France to sink any British vessel, and while these castaways were not British, they were able-bodied, and Bisset needed men to replace the five who had died of scurvy in the last month. The war, such as it was, waited for no one, and these students, with their dead phones and minor degrees, were about to become footnotes in a logbook that would rot in a French archive by 1750.

The longboat was ten yards from shore. The coxswain shouted again, his voice rough with tobacco and salt. “Identify yourselves, or we fire!”

Liam grinned, thinking it a joke, a reenactor’s bit. He held up the granola bar. “We come in peace! Look, food!”

The narrator watched, cynical and unsurprised. The signal fire flared, a spark catching the hem of Liam’s t-shirt, he slapped it out, laughing, while the French muskets clicked, hammers drawing back. _La gloire est une illusion vendue aux pauvres_, as the rifleman said. The tide turned, the wind shifted, and the past, indifferent as ever, waited to swallow them whole.
