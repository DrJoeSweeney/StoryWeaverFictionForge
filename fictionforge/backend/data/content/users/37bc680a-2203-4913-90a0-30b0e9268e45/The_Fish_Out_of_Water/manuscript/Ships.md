**Caribbean Military Situation, 1710**

The Caribbean in 1710 was less a tropical paradise than a seething cauldron of imperial ambition, naval posturing, and opportunistic violence—where the distinction between legitimate warfare and outright piracy blurred with the frequency of a tropical squall. The War of the Spanish Succession, now entering its tenth year, had transformed the archipelago into a theatre where Europe’s quarrels were settled not in salons or treaty halls, but amid coral reefs, mangrove swamps, and the creaking timbers of overburdened merchantmen. Spain, Britain, France, and the Dutch Republic each maintained a presence—uneven, often overstretched, and perpetually vulnerable to both enemy action and the whims of local governors whose loyalty was as fickle as the trade winds.

Spain, though diminished from its sixteenth-century zenith, still clung fiercely to its *Carrera de Indias*—the lifeline of silver, cochineal, and cacao that wound from Veracruz and Cartagena back to Cádiz. Its naval presence remained concentrated around Havana, the linchpin of Spanish Caribbean defence, where galleons and guardacostas (coastal patrol vessels) prowled with weary vigilance. Yet Madrid’s grip was slipping. The Bourbon claimant to the Spanish throne, Philip V, now wore the crown, but his alliance with France had turned traditional Spanish neutrality into active belligerence—much to the chagrin of British and Dutch merchants who had once traded (however illicitly) with relative impunity.

Britain, meanwhile, was in the ascendant—not yet dominant, but undeniably aggressive. Its colonies in Jamaica, Barbados, and the Leeward Islands served as forward operating bases for both the Royal Navy and a burgeoning class of privateers licensed under letters of marque. Port Royal, though still recovering from the 1692 earthquake, functioned as a hub of naval logistics and illicit commerce alike. British squadrons under commanders like Vice-Admiral Sir John Jennings patrolled the Windward Passage and the Mona Channel, ostensibly to protect convoys but often doubling as instruments of economic warfare against French and Spanish shipping. It was in this climate that Captain Marigold Hale—sharp-eyed, ruthlessly pragmatic—calculated her trade routes not by charts alone, but by the shifting allegiances of admirals and the latest rumours from Martinique. Her brig, the *Meridian*, carried sugar one week and gunpowder the next, depending on which port offered the better margin and the lesser risk of seizure.

France, though battered by continental defeats, retained a formidable Caribbean footprint. Martinique and Guadeloupe remained key strongholds, their governors empowered to issue letters of marque with astonishing liberality. French privateers—many scarcely distinguishable from pirates—operated out of Saint-Pierre and Basse-Terre, preying on British and Dutch merchantmen with cheerful efficiency. The French Navy maintained a modest squadron in the Antilles, though chronic underfunding and the demands of European theatres meant its ships were often in poor repair. Nevertheless, their presence was enough to deter casual encroachment—especially near Saint-Domingue, where the colony’s sugar wealth made it worth defending at all costs.

The Dutch Republic, ever the merchant’s empire, maintained a more circumspect posture. Curaçao and St. Eustatius served as neutral entrepôts—ostensibly, at least—where goods (and intelligence) changed hands beneath fluttering tricolour flags. Dutch naval deployments were minimal; their strength lay in commercial networks, not broadsides. Yet neutrality was a fiction easily shredded by wartime necessity. Dutch merchantmen were routinely stopped, searched, and occasionally seized by all sides, prompting plaintive diplomatic protests that went largely unheeded. In truth, the Dutch relied less on guns than on guile—and on figures like Sir Alaric Zafira, that shadowy broker of whispers who seemed to know which governor had taken Spanish gold, which British commodore was open to bribes, and which French privateer captain could be turned with a well-placed letter and a cask of Madeira. Zafira’s influence was intangible but pervasive; he left no manifest, commanded no vessel, yet ships altered course on his word alone.

Amid this maelstrom sailed the *Fish Out of Water*—a vessel whose very name seemed a wry commentary on its predicament. Displaced from a future it could not explain, crewed by men and women whose knowledge of celestial navigation was matched only by their ignorance of contemporary politics, the ship drifted perilously close to the jagged edges of imperial rivalry. Elizabeth Van Dyne, navigator extraordinary, found herself wrestling not only with the Caribbean’s notorious currents and the unreliability of 1710-era charts, but with the near-impossible task of plotting a course that avoided not just reefs, but warships flying four different flags—each with the legal right (or convenient pretext) to board, impress, or sink her on sight.

Sir Reginald Thorne, ever the opportunist, saw not danger but openings. To him, the fractured loyalties of the Caribbean were not a liability but a menu. He traded in secrets as others traded in molasses: a tip about a Spanish treasure galleon’s schedule here, a forged letter of marque there. He cultivated contacts in Port Royal, Havana, and even the back alleys of Cap-Français, all while maintaining a veneer of gentlemanly respectability that fooled precisely no one who mattered—and everyone who didn’t. His survival depended on staying one step ahead of the hangman and two steps ahead of his creditors, a balancing act he performed with unnerving panache.

For the crew of the *Fish Out of Water*, every port held equal promise and peril. A friendly-looking schooner might belong to a British privateer eager to impress able seamen; a seemingly abandoned cay might conceal a French shore battery; a chance encounter with a Dutch trader could lead to salvation—or betrayal. The war provided cover for all manner of irregular activity, but it also meant that no act of violence required justification beyond “the enemy did it first.”

In sum, the Caribbean of 1710 was a place where legality was situational, loyalty was transactional, and survival depended less on firepower than on knowing who to bribe, when to run, and which rumours to believe. For a ship that didn’t belong in this century—or any century of sail—the only true compass was caution. And even that, as Elizabeth Van Dyne well knew, was no guarantee against shipwreck.
