The first thing Eleanor Blackwood noticed was the sound of waves. This was wrong. The last thing she remembered was the hum of the LHC, the particular vibration that got into your teeth and stayed there, and Dr. Vasquez's voice saying something about quantum states and probability matrices. Not waves. Not the cry of birds she did not recognise.

The silence stretched between them like a blade between the ribs. Eleanor's knuckles whitened around the haft of her walking stick—not that it would do much good against whatever had been scratching at the perimeter fence, or the men who had come looking for them three days past with dogs and torches and questions that turned cruel when the answers proved unsatisfactory.

"It's not the waiting I mind," she said finally, shifting her weight from one swollen ankle to the other. "It's the wondering."

"Wondering what?"

"Wondering if we're the prey or the bait."

The jungle exhaled around them in great, wet breaths. Vines dripped with the patience of centuries, and somewhere in that green hell, something large enough to make the ground tremble was moving with purpose. Eleanor had stopped trying to identify the sounds two weeks ago—gunshots, screams, the occasional burst of what might have been laughter, or prayer, or both. On an island like this, with a population that had grown exponentially since the mutiny, distinctions blurred like faces in a fever dream.

"They'll come for us eventually," the man said, though whether he meant rescue or reprisal, even he couldn't have said. "They always do."

Eleanor snorted, a sound like stones tumbling in a dry creek bed. "You think too highly of them. Most folks prefer their problems solved at a distance, with paperwork and promotions." She tapped her stick against the rotting planks of the dock. "Besides, we made sure of that when we burned the radio."

The memory of that night still lived in her dreams—the crackle of flames consuming their only link to the outside world, the smell of burning insulation mixing with the salt air, the way her companion's hands had shaken as he hurled the transmitter into the sea. Heroic, she supposed, if one believed in such things. Desperate, if one didn't.

The howler monkey's cry split the humid air like a rusty hinge, and both of them jerked. Not that they weren't accustomed to the jungle's cacophony—the constant drone of insects, the screech of parrots, the wet slap of something dying in the undergrowth—it was simply that this particular shriek had evolved into something more precise than mere animal noise. Three o'clock precisely, give or take five minutes, and the daily reckoning began anew.

Thomas wiped sweat from his eyes with the back of a hand that had been clean three weeks ago, when such niceties as personal hygiene hadn't yet become a luxury. The gesture left a streak of mud and something unidentifiable across his cheekbone, but he barely noticed anymore. His shirt clung to his back like a second skin, soaked through with salt and the sour tang of his own body odour—a scent that had somehow become comforting in its familiarity.

Eleanor sat a few metres away, methodically arranging their meagre supplies with the precision of a woman who had once calibrated laboratory equipment to tolerances of microns. Now she was organising broken seashells and shards of mirror, trying to make the most of their salvage, but even her clinical mind couldn't disguise the fact that they were essentially playing shopkeeper with garbage. The irony wasn't lost on either of them; Eleanor's speciality had been pharmacology, and she could probably synthesise antibiotics from local flora if she had the proper equipment, sterile conditions, and about six months of uninterrupted research time. Instead, she was making a careful inventory of their collective failure under a canopy that dripped with humidity so thick it felt like breathing through a wet cloth.

The air itself was a living thing here—hot and wet and smelling of decay and growth in equal measure. Rotting vegetation mingled with the sharp, medicinal scent of crushed leaves wherever they'd disturbed the undergrowth, and beneath it all lay the island's underlying sweetness, a perfume that spoke of life abundant and utterly indifferent to human suffering. Somewhere nearby, water trickled—a sound that should have been hopeful, but wasn't, because they'd already followed every stream to its salty end and found nothing drinkable.

Thomas's stomach growled, a sound that seemed obscenely loud in the sudden quiet that followed the monkey's proclamation. It was a hollow, insistent noise that spoke of twelve days without proper food, of coconuts that tasted of nothing but fibre, of fish they couldn't catch and fruit that either disagreed with them violently or simply didn't exist in sufficient quantities. He'd lost twenty pounds, which should have pleased him, given his usual battles with weight, but instead just made him feel like a collection of bones wrapped in damp paper.

"Right then," Eleanor said, her voice carrying that particular tone of British resignation that suggested she was about to deliver news of terminal disappointment. "Water situation?"

"Two days, maybe three if we're careful. The catchment system's working, but we're losing more to evaporation than I'd like." Thomas gestured toward their jury-rigged arrangement of leaves and bamboo, an engineering solution that would have embarrassed a first-year student back home but represented the pinnacle of his current capabilities. "And we still haven't figured out how to make the solar still work properly without proper glass."

Eleanor nodded, making a notation on a piece of salvaged paper with a pencil stub that was rapidly becoming too short for comfort. "Medical supplies?"

"Bandages are holding up. I've been using the cleaner parts of our shirts. The antiseptic situation is... fluid." He didn't elaborate on the fact that his definition of antiseptic had expanded to include anything that didn't immediately cause gangrene.

Above them, the monkey shrieked again, either in response to their conversation or simply because it was a howler monkey and that was what it did. Either way, the message was clear: time was passing, resources were dwindling, and rescue remained as distant a possibility as it had been on day one. The island kept its own schedule, measured in tides and animal calls rather than clocks, and it was a schedule that grew increasingly indifferent to their presence.

Thomas looked out across the lagoon, its surface mottled with oil from their wrecked vessel and the occasional ripple that suggested something large and unfriendly lived in the deeper water. The Fish Out of Water—God, what a cruel joke that name had become—lay broken on the reef like a discarded toy, her timbers already being colonised by the relentless growth of the tropics. She'd saved them once, that much was certain, but she'd also delivered them into a paradise that was rapidly revealing itself as something closer to purgatory.

"Shelter?" Eleanor asked, continuing her grim liturgy.

"Leaking. Again. And I'm reasonably certain something has taken up residence in the back corner. I can hear it moving around at night." Thomas paused, then added with what he hoped sounded like casual detachment, "Also, I think I'm developing a rash. Possibly from the mould that's starting to grow on everything, possibly from the stress, possibly from the fact that I haven't had a proper bath in nearly two weeks."

Eleanor looked up from her list, her expression one of professional assessment that he recognised from their university days—the same look she'd worn when examining bacterial cultures or evaluating the efficacy of various drug compounds. Only now, instead of a laboratory, she was cataloguing their slow descent into savagery with the same methodical precision she'd once applied to peer-reviewed research.

"Well," she said finally, folding the paper carefully despite its increasing illegibility, "tomorrow's another day."

Thomas barked a laugh that sounded more like a cough. "In which we'll probably die of dehydration, exposure, or whatever's living in our shelter. Yes, quite right. Chin up and all that."

The howler monkey seemed to agree, launching into another round of throaty proclamations that echoed through the green cathedral of the jungle. It was, Thomas reflected, the closest thing they had to a church bell, marking the hours of their slow pilgrimage toward whatever came next. Whether that was salvation or simply the end mattered less than the fact that they were still here to mark it at all.

"How's the leg?" he asked, though they both knew she wouldn't have admitted to the injury if it had been mortal.

"Functional."

"That's one word for it."

Eleanor looked down at her swollen ankle, already turning the colour of old bruises. "It's the only word that matters. Pain is just information, and information is just another kind of currency here." She'd learned that lesson from the missionary's wife, before the woman had decided that starvation was preferable to sharing her remaining biscuits. Eleanor had disagreed violently, and now she was limping.

The man—she'd stopped remembering his name sometime after the second week—was watching the tree line with the intensity of a man expecting execution. Which, she supposed, they both were. Just a matter of timing and theatrical preference.

"You hear that?" he whispered.

Eleanor strained her ears. At first, nothing but the usual symphony of insects and distant water. Then—a rhythm. Footsteps. Many footsteps. Not the careful tread of hunters or the shuffling gait of the wounded, but something more purposeful. Military, maybe. Or merely organized.

"Well," she said, raising her stick like a sceptre, "if we're to be rescued, rescued we shall be. If we're to be murdered, murdered we shall be. Either way, standing here looking nervous won't improve our chances."

But as the sounds drew closer, resolving into the unmistakable cadence of marching boots and the occasional barked command, Eleanor found herself thinking not of rescue or revenge, but of tea. Proper tea, with milk and sugar, served in a porcelain cup that hadn't been washed in recycled ditch water. Of clean sheets and hot baths and the particular luxury of boredom.

How French of her, she thought bitterly, to romanticise civilisation at the moment of her potential salvation. Or damnation. On this island, the distinction had become academic.

The first figure emerged from the jungle like a ghost stepping from a nightmare, followed by others, their uniforms crisp despite the humidity, their weapons held with the casual professionalism of people who had forgotten the meaning of fear. Eleanor straightened, planted her stick firmly in the rotting wood, and prepared to meet whatever came next with the dignity of someone who had already died a dozen deaths in her imagination.

It was, she reflected, the only preparation that ever seemed to help.

She opened her eyes to a sky the colour of a week-old bruise, filtered through canvas that smelled of salt and something vaguely organic that might have been mildew or regret. The deck beneath her shifted with the grace of a drunkard, and the air carried the rich bouquet of seaweed, unwashed humanity, and a hint of bilge water that spoke of poor decisions and poorer plumbing.

Her head throbbed with the precision of a carpenter's mallet, and when she tried to sit up, the world demonstrated its disagreement with a spectacular display of spinning that would have impressed a dervish. Somewhere nearby, a man cursed in what sounded like French and then Portuguese and then possibly sailor, his voice as rough as the rope that cut into her cheek where she'd been lying.

The last thing she remembered was sterile white lights and the taste of copper, but the present offered no such comforts—only the creak of wood that had never known power tools, the distant cry of seabirds that hadn't evolved to avoid oil tankers, and the unsettling realisation that her mobile phone was not just dead but centuries out of place.

She closed her eyes again, briefly, just to confirm that reality was still there when she wasn't looking at it.

She closed her eyes again, briefly, just to confirm that reality was still there when she wasn't looking at it.

Green. Everything was green and wet and aggressively alive. A leaf the size of her forearm hovered inches from her face, trembling with the effort of supporting a drop of water that caught the light like a crystal ball. She could see her own reflection in it, distorted, a woman she almost recognised with leaves in her hair and a bruise on her cheek she did not remember earning.

She sat up. The world spun, then steadied. Sand beneath her—not the grey industrial grit of Geneva, but something pale and fine that stuck to her wet skin. Palm trees. Actual palm trees, their fronds rattling in a breeze that smelled of salt and something sweeter underneath, something organic and decaying that she tried not to think about too carefully.

Her clothes were wrong. She was still wearing the white lab coat, but it was torn now, the left sleeve ripped from shoulder to elbow, and underneath she wore a t-shirt she did not recognise—grey, with a faded logo she couldn't quite read. Her trousers were intact, but her shoes were gone. Her feet were bare and covered in sand.

"Eleanor?"

She turned. Thomas Carver stood at the edge of her vision, half-hidden behind a trunk of something tropical and enormous. He looked worse than she felt, which was saying something. His dark hair was matted on one side, and there was blood—dried now, rust-brown, running from a gash above his left eye down to his jaw. He was barefoot like her, and his lab coat was missing entirely, replaced by a blue shirt she had never seen before.

"Thomas." Her voice came out cracked, foreign. She cleared her throat. "Where—"

"I don't know." He said it flatly, without emotion, which was how Thomas always delivered news. He was a physicist, not a poet; he dealt in facts, even when the facts were useless. "I woke up here. Twenty minutes ago, maybe. I've been walking in circles. There's—" He stopped, pressed a hand to his temple. "There's something wrong with my memory."

"What do you mean?"

"The experiment." He said it like a question. "CERN. The project. I remember—we were in the control room, all of us, the ten of us, and Vasquez was running the final sequence. I remember that much. But after—" He shook his head. "Nothing. There's a gap. Like a tape that's been cut."

Eleanor felt something cold move through her chest. She tried to remember and found only fog, thick and impenetrable, where the experiment's conclusion should have been. She remembered the morning. She remembered coffee that was too bitter and Martinez making a joke about quantum entanglement that nobody understood. She remembered Vasquez's face, tight with concentration, his eyes fixed on the monitors. She remembered the countdown.

Then nothing.

Then palm trees. Then waves. Then Thomas Carver looked at her like she was the only solid thing in a world that had suddenly become liquid and wrong.

"Other people," she said. "You said ten of us. Where are the others?"

"I don't know." He said it again, and this time there was something underneath the flatness—fear, maybe, or its close cousin. "I've been calling out. Nobody answered. But this island isn't big. I think—I think I saw smoke, earlier. On the other side. Could be them."

"Could be something else."

"Yes." He looked at her feet, then at his own. "We should find shoes. Something. I don't know what's in that jungle, but I don't want to meet it barefoot."

They walked. The jungle was denser than it looked from the beach, a wall of green that pressed in from all sides, and the noise—Eleanor had not expected the noise. Birds she couldn't see, calling to each other in voices that sounded almost human. Insects humming at a frequency that made her teeth ache. The constant shush of wind through leaves, like the island was breathing, and she could not decide if that was comforting or the opposite.

Thomas found a stick, handed it to her. She took it without thanks; there was no time for manners when the world had stopped making sense. They moved through the undergrowth slowly, carefully, following what might have been a trail or might have been a gap where something large had pushed through recently. The ground was soft, damp, and twice she felt things move beneath her feet that she chose not to examine too closely.

"Blackwood," Thomas said, after what might have been twenty minutes or an hour—time had become as unreliable as everything else. "When you woke up. Did you feel—anything? Strange?"

"Besides the obvious?"

"Besides the obvious."

She thought about it. The fog in her mind, yes. The wrongness of her clothes. The bruise on her cheek she could not explain. But underneath that, something else. Something that felt like static, like the afterimage of a sound too loud to hear properly.

"I feel watched," she said finally. "Like there's something in my head that's not supposed to be there. Like I dreamed something I can't remember, and the dream is still dreaming me."

Thomas was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Yes. That's exactly it."

They found the first body ten minutes later.

It was not a body, not quite. The man was alive, curled at the base of a tree, his arms wrapped around his knees and his eyes open and fixed on nothing. He was wearing clothes that did not belong to him—a linen shirt, torn, that looked like it belonged on a sailing ship, and trousers that were too short in the leg and too wide in the waist. His feet were bare and cut, the wounds already beginning to swell.

"James," Thomas said. "James Okonkwo."

The man did not respond. Did not seem to hear them. Eleanor crouched beside him, close enough to smell him—sweat and salt and something underneath, something sour and wrong.

"James. James, can you hear me?"

His eyes moved. Slowly, like a camera adjusting focus, they tracked to her face. His lips parted. No sound came out. Then, with visible effort:

"Blackwood. Carver. You're here." A pause, longer than it should have been. "You're here. That's good. That's—I thought I was the only one. I thought—"

He stopped. His eyes rolled back, and he slumped sideways, unconscious.

Eleanor looked at Thomas. Thomas looked at Eleanor. The thing in her head that felt like static hummed louder, and she had the sudden, irrational certainty that whatever had happened to them, whatever had torn them out of Geneva and deposited them on this island with its wrong air and its wrong light and its wrong everything, it was not finished with them yet.

"We need to find the others," she said.

"Yes," Thomas agreed. He was staring at James's face, at the cuts on his feet, at the clothes that belonged to no one. "But Blackwood—look at him. Look at his feet. Those cuts. They're not from walking. They're from something else. Something sharp."

"I saw. I don't want to think about what."

"Neither do I. But we should. We should think about it very carefully." He helped her to her feet, and they left James Okonkwo where he lay, breathing but not awake, wounded but not bleeding, a physicist from Lagos who had somehow ended up on a tropical island wearing clothes from another century.

The jungle pressed in around them, green and wet and alive, and somewhere in the distance, the smoke Thomas had seen earlier was still rising, a thin grey line against a sky that was the wrong colour, too bright, too blue, like a painting of a sky rather than a real one.

Eleanor gripped her stick tighter and kept walking. Behind her, the waves continued their endless, indifferent rhythm, and the island, she was certain, was watching them go.

* * *

The smoke led them to a clearing, and the clearing led them to chaos.

There were five others already there, or what remained of five others. Priya Sharma was sitting on a fallen log, her dark hair hanging limp around her face, her hands wrapped around a bottle of water that she stared at like it might explain everything. She looked up when Eleanor and Thomas emerged from the trees, and her expression—Eleanor would remember that expression for a long time. It was the look of someone who had stopped expecting rescue.

"Blackwood. Carver." Priya's voice was hoarse. "You're the seventh and eighth. We were starting to think we were alone."

"James is unconscious," Eleanor said. "In the jungle, maybe ten minutes back. He's hurt."

"I know. I found him." Priya's hands tightened on the bottle. "I tried to wake him. He said—something. About a sound. About something that came out of the air."

"What kind of sound?"

"I don't know. He couldn't describe it. He just said—" She stopped, swallowed. "He said it was like being unmade. Like every atom in his body forgot what it was supposed to be doing."

The static in Eleanor's head pulsed, and she staggered, caught herself on a tree trunk. Thomas's hand was on her arm, steadying her, and for a moment the world was nothing but green and grey and the overwhelming smell of vegetation rotting in the heat.

"Vasquez," she said, when she could speak again. "Dr. Vasquez. Was he—"

"Here?" Priya shook her head. "No. We've counted. All ten of us were supposed to be in that room. All ten of us were in the observation deck when the sequence started. But when we woke up—" She gestured at the clearing, at the five people sitting or standing or pacing in various states of confusion and distress. "There are only eight of us so far. Vasquez and Chen are missing."

"Missing," Thomas repeated. "You mean—"

"I mean, they're not here. I mean, we've searched as much as we can search, and there's no sign of them. I mean—" Priya's voice cracked, and she looked away. "I mean, they were standing right next to me, and now they're gone, and I don't know what happened to them, and I don't know what's happening to us, and I don't know how to make it stop."

The clearing was quiet for a moment. Someone—Eleanor didn't recognise them at first, until she did, until she saw the familiar set of the shoulders and the way the hair fell across the forehead—Marcus Webb, the Australian, the one who always laughed too loud in the seminars—someone was crying, softly, the kind of crying that didn't want to be heard. Someone else was laughing, a high, brittle sound that broke off into silence.

Eleanor looked at Thomas. Thomas looked at Eleanor. The static in her head was getting louder, and she could see in his eyes that he felt it too, whatever it was, this wrongness that had no name.

"We need to find Vasquez," she said. "We need to find Chen. And we need to figure out what happened to us."

"And if we can't?" Priya asked.

Eleanor didn't have an answer for that. She didn't have an answer for any of it—why they were here, why their memories stopped at the same moment, why James Okonkwo was barefoot and bleeding and talking about being unmade. She didn't know why the sky was the wrong colour or why the air tasted like metal or why every instinct she had was screaming at her to run, even though there was nowhere to run to.

But she knew one thing. Whatever had happened, whatever had torn them out of CERN and dropped them on this island that shouldn't exist, it was not an accident. It was not a malfunction. Something had done this to them, and whatever that something was, it was still here, watching from the trees, waiting for something she couldn't begin to imagine.

"We'll figure it out," she said, because someone had to say something, because silence was worse than lies. "We're physicists. We solve problems. This is just a problem we haven't solved yet."

Thomas made a sound that might have been a laugh. "That's what you think. That this is a problem."

"What else would it be?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. They both knew what it was. It was not a problem. It was a sentence, and they were still waiting to learn what they had done to deserve it.

The island breathed around them, green and wet and patient, and somewhere in the jungle, something moved that was not any of them. Eleanor gripped her stick and waited for the next thing to go wrong, because at this point, waiting was all she could do.

It didn't take too long.
