# Chapter Two: The Twelve Days

The island had no name, only the stink of salt and something rotten beneath it. Eleanor knelt in the brackish water, her boots sinking into the mud, a handful of seaweed between her fingers. The water was warm and foul, a soup of dead things and tidal leavings, and she had been at it for three hours without finding anything that would not make them sicker than they already were. Her fingers had gone pruney and white, the skin soft enough to tear, and the cut on her palm from the wreck had reopened without her noticing. The blood threaded through the water like a thought she could not finish.

Thomas watched from the ridge, arms crossed, chewing on a blade of grass that had long since lost any claim to flavour. His hat hung off one ear, and the sweat under his collar had already soaked through to his ribs. He had taken off his shoes an hour ago and now regretted it; the sand had worked its way into the weave of his socks like ground glass. He did not mention this. Complaining was a currency, and they were both bankrupt.

“You’ll drown the lot of us,” he said, voice flat as a plate. “Last time you ‘foraged,’ you brought back a conch the size of a tambourine. It bit me.”

“It was _defending itself_,” she shot back, rinsing the algae off her palms with a violence that suggested the algae had personally offended her. “Besides, you never said anything when I found the mushrooms that smelled like old socks. You ate four of them and complained about the texture for an hour.”

“The texture was appalling.”

“You’re appalling.”

He stood, brushing mud from his knees, a gesture of dignity that served no purpose because the mud was everywhere and would be everywhere until they died or were rescued, whichever came first. The map they had carried from the sinking lifeboat fluttered in the wind, pinned under a stone that was not quite heavy enough. Neither of them could read it. It might as well have been a shopping list from Carthage.

The first day passed in the manner of all first days after a disaster: busy, pointless, and punctuated by argument. Thomas built a shelter from driftwood and seaweed, muttering about load-bearing angles and tensile strength as though the island were an engineering examination and he intended to pass with honours. The roof collapsed twice before noon. The third time, he simply sat beneath it and let the seaweed drape over his shoulders like a defeated laurel. Eleanor rolled her eyes with the full weight of someone who had spent three years at a women’s college learning to roll her eyes at men who built things that fell down.

“You’re going to build yourself a cabin before we eat?” she said, picking apart a tangle of kelp with her fingernails. The kelp was rubbery and uncooperative, like everything else on the island. “I admire the ambition. Truly. When the Germans find us, they’ll find a corpse in a very well-constructed house.”

“Priorities,” he snapped. The wood splintered under his hands. A gull squawked overhead, a sound of pure contempt. Thomas looked up at it and considered throwing a stone, but the gull was already gone, and his arm was too tired to lift anyway.

By noon, the sun had turned the sand to a blistering sheen that hurt to look at. Eleanor scraped barnacles off a dead crab, her fingers numb despite the heat. The crab had been dead for some time, and the barnacles came away reluctantly, leaving white scars on the shell. She pried one loose and held it up to the light. It was no bigger than a fingernail, grey and unlovely, but it was protein, or something close enough to protein that the body might be fooled. She dropped it into her palm and kept scraping.

Thomas lay on his back in the shade of his collapsed shelter, staring at the sky with the intensity of a man trying to bore a hole through it. “This is what they meant by ‘survival of the fittest,’” he said, to no one in particular. “Fittest for what? For _dying_? Because I feel I have made a credible start. I am dying of hunger, dying of thirst, and dying of the specific humiliation of being lectured about architecture by a woman who once ate a mushroom that turned her tongue blue.”

“It was an experiment,” Eleanor said, without looking up. “And the blueness passed.”

A silence stretched between them, the long elastic silence of two people who had run out of things to say and were now simply enduring the presence of each other. Then Eleanor stood abruptly, clutching a limp kelp frond as though it were a royal standard. “We need fire. Or a signal. Or—”

“A bloody dell,” Thomas interrupted, snatching the frond from her hand and tearing it apart with his teeth. The salt stung his lips. He chewed mechanically, swallowing against the gag reflex. “We’re not scouts. We’re not heroes. We’re _lunch_ if someone finds us.” He shoved the rest of the mess into his mouth and lay back down, staring at the sky. The taste of salt and despair, he decided, was not improved by repetition.

But survival, as it turned out, did not require agreement. It required only the refusal to die in the same hour.

* * *

The second day, Eleanor discovered the trick of the tide pools. They lay along the eastern edge of the island, cupped in black rock like bowls set out for an absent god. The water in them was clearer than the marsh, warmed by the sun, and alive with small translucent things that flicked and darted. She spent the morning on her knees, scooping up what she could with her cupped hands and dropping it into a hollowed coconut shell that Thomas had split with a sharp rock and a great deal of cursing. The catch was meagre—a dozen shrimp no bigger than her thumbnail, a crab that had lost three legs and its will to live, something that might have been a fish or might have been a very ambitious worm—but it was food. She brought it back to the camp and divided it into two equal portions with the solemnity of a priestess.

Thomas ate his share without comment, which was as close to gratitude as he ever came. Afterwards, he walked the perimeter of the island three times, marking the high-tide line with piles of stone. He had decided, sometime in the night, that the first requirement of survival was to know the shape of the prison. The island was small—perhaps half a mile across at its widest point—and shaped like a crooked thumb. Low scrub covered most of it, wiry bushes that smelled of pepper and dust, and there was a single stand of trees at the southern end, bent sideways by the wind. The whole thing could be walked in an hour, provided you did not stop to feel sorry for yourself. Thomas did not stop.

The third day, it rained. They collected the water in the sailcloth Thomas had salvaged from the lifeboat, rigging it between two sticks so that the runoff trickled into a salvaged tin cup. The cup filled slowly, drop by grudging drop, and they took turns drinking from it until their stomachs cramped. Eleanor used the rainwater to wash the salt from her hair, and Thomas looked away while she did it, because some privacies remained, even here.

The fourth day, they found the signal fire.

It was Eleanor’s idea, born of the memory that ships still passed this stretch of water, though not often, and not reliably, and not with any interest in two castaways on a nameless spit of sand. She gathered driftwood and dried seaweed and piled it at the highest point of the island, then spent an hour trying to ignite it with the lens from Thomas’s broken compass. The lens was small and scratched, and the sun was uncooperative, sliding behind clouds whenever she got close. Thomas watched from the shelter, offering encouragement in the form of observations about the futility of all human endeavour. When the fire finally caught, he stood up and added his own contribution: a green branch that sent up a column of thick white smoke.

“There,” he said. “Now we are visible. To everyone. Including people who might want to shoot us.”

“At this point,” Eleanor said, “I would welcome the conversation.”

The fifth day, they argued about the Frenchman. He had not returned, and Thomas was of the opinion that he would not, being French and therefore unreliable. Eleanor thought he might, being French and therefore arrogant enough to keep a promise made to strangers. Neither of them was right, which was typical, but the argument kept them occupied through the afternoon, and occupation was its own form of survival.

On the sixth day, Thomas caught a fish with his bare hands. It was a small fish, silver and stupid, trapped in a tide pool that was shrinking in the afternoon heat. He grabbed it before he could think about it, and the feel of it thrashing in his grip was so surprising that he almost let it go. Instead, he dashed its head against a rock and brought it back to Eleanor, holding it out like a boy with a school prize. She looked at the fish. She looked at Thomas. She took the fish and gutted it with the knife she had been sharpening on a stone for three days, and they ate it raw, in small bites, watching the sun go down.

The seventh day, Eleanor became ill. It was the water, she thought, or the crab, or simply the cumulative weight of everything that had happened. Thomas sat with her through the night, and in the morning she was better, or better enough, and they went on.

On the eighth day, they saw the ship. It was far out, a grey shape against the grey horizon, and their signal fire was too low and too thin and the smoke scattered in the wind before it could rise high enough. They watched it pass in silence. Afterwards, Thomas rebuilt the fire, piling on more wood, more green branches, more of everything that might burn. He did not say why. Eleanor did not ask.

The ninth day, they stopped counting.

The tenth day, the Frenchman came back.

* * *

At dusk, they heard the sound—a crash, then a shout in French. Thomas froze. Eleanor clutched a stick like a weapon, the same stick she had been carrying for three days, whittled to a point that was more symbolic than functional.

A man emerged from the trees, a rifle slung over his shoulder, a cigarette dangling from his lips. He paused, surveying the wreckage of their camp with the detached curiosity of a naturalist examining a particularly unimpressive species of beetle.

“Bloody hell,” he muttered. “Lost already?”

Eleanor stood, waving a half-eaten crab that had been her dinner and Thomas’s dinner and was now, she supposed, evidence of their competence. “We’re from the _Antelope_. You sunk us.”

The man snorted. “No, you _jumped_ from it. Like a bloody frog. I saw the whole thing. Very dramatic. Very wet.” He kicked a rock, which skittered into the fire and sent up a shower of sparks. “Come on. The French are this way. Main force, camped on the north shore. We’ll get you to the mainland. In exchange for…” He paused, eyeing her kelp, which she had been drying on a flat stone in the hope that it might become something other than kelp. “A demonstration. Pharmacology, was it?”

Thomas stepped forward, face red with a fury that had been simmering for ten days and now boiled over. “We don’t need your help. We have a fire. We have shelter. We have—”

“A pile of sticks and a fish bone,” the Frenchman finished. “Yes. I saw. Very resourceful. The Germans will be deeply impressed. They will write a report. ‘Encountered two castaways,’ it will say, ‘who had almost learned to feed themselves. Shot them anyway.’” He lit a fresh cigarette from the stub of the old one, a trick of economy that suggested long practice. “You think your little island is safe? The Germans are coming. This stretch of coast will be crawling with them by Tuesday. You’ll die of boredom before they do, but they will certainly hurry the process along.”

He tossed a flare into the sky. It arced up, trailing red, and burst with a crack that echoed off the water. The light hung for a moment, then fell, hissing, into the sea. It left an afterimage on their retinas, a bruise of light that faded slowly.

“But maybe you’re better at _fire_,” the Frenchman said, and smiled. It was not a kind smile, but it was not entirely unkind either.

Eleanor stared at the flare’s charred remains, still smoking on the water. Thomas lit a match, the flame guttering in the wind, cupping it in his palm with the tenderness of a man holding something precious. The match burned down to his fingers before he shook it out.

“French,” he said.

“French _and_ insufferable,” Eleanor muttered. She bent and scooped up the dried kelp, folding it carefully into the pocket of her salt-stiffened jacket. “Fine. But if you’re going to die of starvation on the way, at least let me _try_ to poison you first. I have been practising.”

The man laughed, a sound like shattering glass, loud and sharp and full of edges. “Jeunes filles. Always so… _determined_.”

* * *

Night fell. The stars offered no comfort, but they were familiar now, and familiarity was something. Thomas built the fire higher, his hands steadier than they had been in days. Eleanor tested a handful of berries she had gathered that morning, red and waxy, spat them out when the bitterness hit her tongue, and tossed the rest into the flames. They popped and blackened.

“You ever think about how stupid this is?” Thomas said, staring into the coals. “How we’re just… ants with delusions of grandeur? Building our little mounds, waving our little signals, and the universe doesn’t even bother to step on us. It just lets us run around until we drop.”

Eleanor didn’t answer. Her eyes were on the water, where something dark moved—a shadow, a fish, a memory. After a while, she said, “Ants survive. Colonies last for years. Decades. They build empires under the dirt and no one notices until the kitchen’s full of them.”

Thomas considered this. “So we’re ants who’ve avoided the kitchen.”

“For now.”

When the first ghost ship passed on the horizon—a silhouette, no lights, moving with the slow purpose of something that knew where it was going—they did not look up. They had seen enough miracles to know they were not the miraculous kind.

They slept in the mud that night, backs to each other, hands clutching knives and the memories of home that no longer mattered. The fire died somewhere in the small hours, and the island swallowed them whole, as it had swallowed everything else that had ever washed up on its shore.

In the morning, the Frenchman was still there, sitting on a rock with his rifle across his knees and a fresh cigarette between his teeth. He had made coffee, somehow, in a tin pot that had not been there the night before. The smell of it was the most extraordinary thing Eleanor had ever experienced.

“You are still alive,” he observed. “I am impressed. Most people would have died by now.”

“We’re not most people,” Thomas said, accepting a cup of coffee with hands that shook only slightly.

“No,” the Frenchman agreed. “You are not.” He poured a second cup for Eleanor and watched her drink it with an expression that might have been respect or might have been the anticipation of a debt still to be collected. “Now,” he said, when the coffee was gone, “we go north. The war, unfortunately, will not wait for you to finish your holiday.”

They gathered their things—the knife, the kelp, the map they still could not read—and followed him into the trees. The island released them reluctantly, the mud sucking at their boots, the salt wind pushing at their backs. By noon, they had reached a cove where a boat was waiting, and by dusk, they were somewhere else entirely, which was, after all, the only thing they had wanted in the first place.
